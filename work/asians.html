The story in short

A small family restaurant had no online ordering, and language barriers led to wrong orders. We listened to guests and staff, sketched five early concepts and built a prototype. We tested it with at least ten guests. Then the pandemic closed the restaurant before we built the app.

My contribution

My background: B.S. Computer Science, M.S. Information Science (HCI).

What I did

  • Led the project and the team
  • Ran the survey and interviews with guests and staff
  • Built the interactive prototype in Adobe XD and tested it with 10+ guests

The team and others

  • A UX researcher and a visual designer worked with me
  • I was the lead designer, researcher, and two others just helped me with surveys and collecting user information

Overview

Asian’s is a Berkeley restaurant with no online ordering. Language barriers caused wrong orders. As a volunteer project, I led a small team to design a mobile ordering app.

  1. 01

    User research

    A survey, interviews with guests and staff, and a persona.

  2. 02

    Wireframes

    Five early sketch concepts, each based on what we learned.

  3. 03

    Design system

    Type, color, spacing, and components built from scratch.

  4. 04

    Prototype and testing

    An Adobe XD prototype, usability tests, and card sorting.

The core constraint

Many guests are not comfortable ordering aloud in English. The app had to work without talking.

01Research

Survey

I shared a short Google Form on social media and got about 50 answers. Each answer led to a design choice.

90%want their favorite restaurants to have a mobile app. So I designed a simple, clear ordering flow.
77%are fine using only English, but some still want language options. So I added a language selector.
100%want recommendations for popular dishes. So I added a Popular Items section.
70%are interested in loyalty rewards. So I added a loyalty badge and a credit system.

About Emma

Emma is 32 and lives in Fremont, California. She moved from Italy in 2020. She only eats at a nearby restaurant that serves her favorite food.

She struggles with English and avoids face-to-face talk. She opens the Asian’s app, orders carryout, and picks it up when ready.

Needs
  • An app that offers all the food items
  • Helps people who do not speak English
  • Easier to order, so no waiting
Preferences
  • Support for different languages
  • Easy to use, especially for older people
  • A loyalty program
Goals
  • Use the app fully
  • An easier payment option that saves the card for next time
  • Avoid waiting time

What Emma taught me

Takeaway 1

Favorites

Regular guests want to reorder the same dishes fast.

Takeaway 2

Clear, fair prices

Guests on a budget need to see what they will pay.

Takeaway 3

Easy categories

Food should be simple to browse, without reading long lists.

Takeaway 4

VIP badge and rewards

Regular guests like to feel recognized for coming back.

03Exploring ideas

Early sketches

Early hypothesis

If this, then that

My hypothesis: if the app showed popular dishes, offered a language option and kept orders short, guests would order without help.

Five early concepts

After feedback, I did not pick one idea. I sketched five very different low-fidelity concepts and showed them to people. Pick a concept to compare.

What this version shows

This concept showed big food photos in a two-column grid with a bottom tab bar. It browsed fast but hid prices and descriptions.

What this version shows

This concept was a text-first menu with category tabs and prices. It was clear like a paper menu, but plain.

What this version shows

This concept put a big popular dishes banner on top and the full list below. It answered the survey request for recommendations.

What this version shows

This concept asked for a language first, then showed three big buttons. It was for guests who struggle with English.

What this version shows

This concept asked one question per screen: category, dish, then confirm. It needed almost no reading but took more taps.

Why familiar patterns

I used familiar ordering patterns so guests would not learn a new interface. In testing, guests preferred the simplest, most direct option. The final design built on concept C, Popular first.

Paper sketches

Then I sketched the full flow on paper: splash screen, onboarding, sign up, login, a home screen and a profile.

Low-fidelity wireframes

05Prototype and build

Sketch to design

See how each screen changed from paper sketch to wireframe to final design.

  1. 01 Splash

    The sketch showed only the logo. The final added an orange letter and a short tap hint.

  2. 02 Onboarding

    The sketch had an image box and one line. The final added an illustration, a tagline, page dots and a Skip button.

  3. 03 Sign up

    The sketch had three fields and a button. The final added clear labels, an orange sign up button, and Facebook and Google options.

  4. 04 Login

    The sketch had two fields. The final reused the sign up pattern and added Forgot password and social login.

  5. 05 Home

    The sketch had a popular row and a dish list. The final added a green header with the address, categories, prices, ratings and add buttons.

  6. 06 Profile

    The sketch had a name and a menu list. The final added a photo, a badge and menu icons.

08Reflection

Still unfinished

Still unfinished

The app never launched, so I could not measure orders or mistakes. I do not know if it would have fixed the problem. Testing gave early signals only.

Takeaways

  1. 01

    Start with user research

    A short survey of about 50 people influenced every major screen.

  2. 02

    Adapt to change

    The restaurant closed before the build, so the project stopped at a prototype. I shared the full process and the prototype instead.

  3. 03

    Design from what people say

    Language, recommendations and rewards came from guests, not my guesses.
